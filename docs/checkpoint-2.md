## 2. Key results

1. **The cost of agility, measured.** Verifying one signature costs about 8,000 gas for ECDSA, 12,700 for P-256 through the precompile, 237,000 for P-256 in contract code, and **8.1 million for ML-DSA-44 in contract code**, about a thousand times ECDSA. Registering one ML-DSA key costs a further 5.3 million gas. An ML-DSA signature is 2,420 bytes against 65.
2. **Agility at the application layer has a hard limit (audit finding M-1).** The registry can retire ECDSA for every instruction to the contracts, but on an EVM ledger a holder's account still moves tokens with the ledger's own ECDSA transaction signature. Only the ledger, or contract accounts, can close that path. On Soroban, contract accounts close it, and the account keeps its address when its scheme changes.
3. **What happens to holders who do not migrate**, shown in a test: they keep their balances but can no longer sign, and only a privileged registrar can restore them. That registrar is the remedy and also a single point of control (finding M-2).
4. **"Valid signature, wrong instruction" twice.** The Binance demonstration does a real EIP-7702 delegation on the local chain: a signing service that checks who is asking but not what is being signed gives an account to attacker code, and the key never leaves the service. A service that accepts only typed requests has nothing to abuse. The Bybit demonstration shows the same failure through a compromised signing interface.
5. **Where losses happen.** In the twelve-case list, no loss came from a broken primitive. Operational compromise of keys or signing accounts for about 79% of listed losses (Binance alone about 59%). Industry-wide H1 2026 figures from TRM Labs point the same way: infrastructure compromises were about 15% of incidents and 76% of losses.
6. **The evidence gap is itself a finding.** For the 14 institutional and central-bank platforms, 47 of 56 cells on who can freeze, who can upgrade, who sets signer policy, and post-quantum plans were not found in public material. Only Canton publishes enough to fill most of its row, and its published cryptography list contains no post-quantum scheme.
7. **Verification.** Six critical properties are covered by fuzzing, invariants and Echidna, four symbolically by Halmos (with vacuity controls) and five formally by Certora on the token. Mutation score is 93.3%; all four non-equivalent survivors are now killed by added tests. Static analysis found no true positive. No critical or high finding.

## 3. Binance as the hook

Confirmed from Binance's own report of 3 November 2026: about US$6.34 billion affected; unauthorised use of an internal wallet signing service; exploitation of the EIP-7702 authorisation mechanism; no single private key existed to revoke the authorisations, because the wallet used MPC; about US$2.85 billion frozen or intercepted. Binance's 30 October update says there was no evidence of a private-key leak.

## 6. Something more important than the brief asked for

The brief frames agility as "can the ledger change its signature scheme". The work suggests the sharper question is **who holds the powers that make a ledger's cryptography matter**: the signer policy (which messages a key will sign), the upgrade key (which can redefine what a signature means), the freeze key (which decides whether a theft is permanent) and the registrar (which decides who may sign at all). The two largest thefts on record went through the first. The Binance case also shows the second at protocol level: an Ethereum upgrade added a message type, and a firm's signing policy did not follow. For most institutional ledgers, public documentation does not say who holds these powers. I propose making that the spine of the article: *the mathematics held; the questions are who can instruct the signer, who can change the rules, and who can freeze the asset.*
